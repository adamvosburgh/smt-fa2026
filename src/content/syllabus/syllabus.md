## Description

**Simulations, Models, Twins** explores modes of simulative technologies and speculates on their potential for practitioners of the built environment. Digital twins, agent-based models, and other types of simulations serve a similar purpose to mock-ups and scale models in traditional architecture practices: there is utility in testing a design in a "real" environment, whether physical or simulated. At the same time, simulations are active agents in shaping the realities they depict, and the degree of "reality" they capture is a continuous source of debate. This course will emphasize hands-on experimentation with simulative technologies as tools for crafting arguments, building worlds, and testing how proposals might perform in the "real" world, while bearing in mind that these representations are partial, situated, and constructed.

The course is divided into two halves:

- In the first, participants work through a sequence of weekly tutorials and assignments, all of which are submitted to and shown on this website. Alongside this, we look closely at a set of [sandboxes](/sandboxes/) built for the course: playable simulations whose assumptions are exposed as controls.
- In the second, participants form groups and develop a final project on a topic of their own choosing, using the methods of the first half. This culminates in two "Show and Tell" sessions at the end of the semester, and a public-facing project page on this website.

No prior programming experience is required, but a willingness to work in code, is. The tutorials provide nearly all of the code you will need, and assignments will involve making tweaks on that code base. If you run in trouble along the way, you can find help either through the course-specific AI assistant, or through office hours.

This course sees the critical use of AI tools as a method of expanding the *technical* ambition of projects, and not as a substitute for having original ideas. From week four, the course teaches you to make software by writing a specification for it rather than by typing the code, otherwise referred to as "vibe-coding."

## Class Requirements and Grading

There are a total of 21 possible points. Attendance for each session (11 points), in-class participation (1 point), assignments 1 through 5 (5 points, 1 each), and the final project (4 points).
19 points and above is a HP, 15 points and above is a P, 12 points and above is a LP, and anything below that is a F.

**Attendance (11 points)**
Attendance at all class sessions is required. Per GSAPP attendance policy, three unexcused absences is an automatic Unofficial Withdrawal (UW).

**In-class participation (1 point)**
You are expected to give engaged and generous participation in class discussions and in critique workshops with your peers.

**Assignments (5 points, 1 point each)**
Assignments are graded on completion and are intended to be limited in scope; experimental; and geared towards learning and creative engagement rather than demonstrating advanced skills. They must answer the prompts laid out in each assignment and use the methods taught in the relevant tutorials. All assignments are submitted to this website; polish of presentation is explicitly not a criterion.

**Final Project + Presentation (4 points)**
Two points for the show and tell presentation, two points for the final submission on the course website.

**Extra Credit (1 point)**
Extra credit will be given for the completion of Assignment 6, and additional extra credit can be assigned by the instructor in the event that a student has a LP or F.

## Learning Objectives

After completing the course participants will:

- Be able to read a simulation, model, or digital twin critically: identify its data, its assumptions, its authors, and its intended and unintended uses
- Work with spatial data in Python: load it, join it, and map it
- Write a rule against a dataset and run it forward through time, and say what the run assumes
- Specify a small piece of software precisely enough for a machine to build it, and check the result against something outside itself
- Develop fluency with web-based visualization as a medium for argument
- Situate simulative technologies within the histories of representation in architecture, urbanism, and computation

## AI Policy

This course makes use of AI tools as a method to expand the ambition of projects, and to make the technical portions of the course possible to those without a programming background. 

The line this course draws is between assistance in understanding and substitution of authorship. Students are welcome to use AI tools, except for the purpose of:

- Producing original writing that the student turns in as their own work. Your gallery texts, descriptions, and briefs must be yours.
- Prompting a LLM to produce data. This will ALWAYS result in fabricated data. If the data you want doesn't exist, collect it, or scale the project to what does.
- Generating an artifact you cannot explain. If you cannot say what your sandbox assumes, what data it runs on, and what rule it steps by, it is not your project.

Work that uses AI in these ways, and in other ways that the instructor determines are counter to the objectives of the class, will not be awarded points. 

## Software

Simulation is not tied to any one particular software. The course privileges code over graphical user interface platforms (like Rhino,) because they are more flexible, more legible and editable to LLMs, and lend themselves to sharing work. 

For the first three weeks we write Python in [Google Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/), which needs nothing installed; instructions for running the same code on your own computer are in [Resources](/resources/local-python/). 

From week four a coding agent (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI) is recommended, but not required. Everything you make is a web page and lives on this site. Knowledge of 3D modeling software will be useful for some final projects but is not required.

## Academic Integrity

The intellectual venture in which we are all engaged requires of faculty and students alike the highest level of personal and academic integrity. As members of an academic community, each one of us bears the responsibility to participate in scholarly discourse and research in a manner characterized by intellectual honesty and scholarly integrity.

Scholarship, by its very nature, is an iterative process, with ideas and insights building one upon the other. Collaborative scholarship requires the study of other scholars' work, the free discussion of such work, and the explicit acknowledgement of those ideas in any work that inform our own. This exchange of ideas relies upon a mutual trust that sources, opinions, facts, and insights will be properly noted and carefully credited.

In practical terms, this means that, as students, you must be responsible for the full citations of others' ideas in all of your research papers and projects; you must be scrupulously honest when taking your examinations; you must always submit your own work and not that of another student, scholar, or internet agent.

Any breach of this intellectual responsibility is a breach of faith with the rest of our academic community. It undermines our shared intellectual culture, and it cannot be tolerated. Students failing to meet these responsibilities should anticipate being asked to leave Columbia.

For more information on academic integrity at Columbia, students may refer to the [Columbia University Undergraduate Guide to Academic Integrity](http://www.college.columbia.edu/academics/academicintegrity) as well as the GSAPP [Honor System](https://www.arch.columbia.edu/honor-system) and [Plagiarism Policy](https://www.arch.columbia.edu/plagiarism-policy).

## Email Policy

Students should not rely on or expect an immediate response to questions sent via email to the instructor. Please begin assignments with enough time to attend office hours or ask a question several days before the assignment is due.

Learning how to troubleshoot technical issues and locate relevant resources is crucial to your long-term success with computational methods. With this in mind, emails with technical questions must at a minimum contain the following:

- a clear description of what you are trying to do, and what the problem is
- a summary of the steps you have already taken to address the issue
- screenshots or error messages (where applicable)
